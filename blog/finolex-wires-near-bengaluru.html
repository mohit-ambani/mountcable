What the map result does and does not tell you

The listing shows a pin, a rating and often the word "Finolex" in the shop's name or description. None of that is an authorisation. Finolex authorises specific distributors and dealers by certificate; it does not police what a shop writes on its map listing. The map answers "who is close?" and leaves "who is genuine?" entirely to you.

A practical way to read the results:

  • "Authorised Finolex distributor" or "dealer" in the name is a claim. Ask to see the certificate. The authorised dealer guide shows what a real one looks like.
  • "Electricals", "hardware", "sanitary and electrical" tells you the shop is general. It may still stock genuine Finolex, but probably through several hands.
  • Reviews tell you whether people liked the service. Nobody writes a review eight years later when a circuit runs warm, so reviews say nothing about copper.

The nearby shop is good for some things

We are not arguing that you should never use the shop near you. For a switch plate, a length of conduit, a holder or a fuse, the nearby shop is faster and the risk is trivial. The nearby shop comparison sets out when each makes sense. Wire is different because you cannot see copper through insulation and the coil is buried for thirty years.

The check that settles it, wherever you buy

Every Finolex carton carries two QR codes. The outer code is printed on the carton label next to the size, grade, length and batch number; scanning it opens Finolex's own verification page and confirms the carton is registered. The inner code is inside the box and can only be reached after opening it; it confirms the contents are what Finolex packed. Both must pass. A genuine carton can be emptied and refilled, in which case the outer code still verifies and only the inner code fails. Do both scans at your own site, before paying.
